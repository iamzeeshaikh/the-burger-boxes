Board is the decision that quietly sets everything else: how much heat the box holds, how
long it resists grease, how it survives a stack in a delivery bag, and what your packaging
says before anyone opens it. Three options cover most burger orders.

## Cardboard: the general-purpose choice

Cardboard is a single layer of paperboard, and it is where most burger boxes start. It
holds its shape for the length of a normal service, takes print cleanly and evenly, and is
the cheapest of the three per unit. For counter service, where the box is filled and handed
over within a minute or two, it is usually all the box that is needed.

Its limit is time and weight. A heavy build sitting in a hot box for twenty minutes will
eventually soften a single layer, which is where the other two come in.

## Corrugated: stiffness and insulation

Corrugated board adds a fluted layer sandwiched between liners. Those flutes do two useful
things: they stiffen the box, so it resists crushing when stacked, and they trap air, which
slows heat loss. That makes corrugated the sensible default for delivery, for heavier or
taller builds, and for anything that has to sit in a bag with other orders on top of it.

It costs more per unit and takes up more space flat, so it is worth paying for where the
journey is long and hard to justify where it is not. The range is grouped under
[burger boxes by material](/product-category/boxes-by-material/).

## Kraft: unbleached, and it looks it

Kraft is paperboard left unbleached, which is why it arrives brown. Structurally it behaves
much like cardboard; the difference is what it communicates. Brown kraft reads as plain,
unprocessed and deliberate, which is why so many independents use it and why it needs
almost no print to look considered — a single-color logo on kraft often looks better than
full-color artwork on white.

## Where the others fit

Plain paper board is lighter and cheaper again, and suits smaller items where structure
matters less than cost. Bagasse is pressed from the sugarcane fiber left over after milling;
it is sturdy, resists grease without a plastic lining, and composts, which makes it the
option to look at when the environmental claim is genuinely part of the brand rather than a
line on the website.

## How to choose without overthinking it

Start with the journey. If the box is handed over the counter and eaten within minutes,
cardboard or kraft is enough and the choice between them is aesthetic. If it goes into a
delivery bag, move to corrugated and stop worrying about crushing. If your customers care
about the material and you can say something true about it, bagasse or recycled stock earns
its place.

Then check the build. A tall stacked burger with a lot of sauce puts more stress on the
board than a slider does, and the heavier the build the more a stiffer box pays for itself
in orders that arrive intact.

## Board and print together

Board also changes how print looks. White cardboard gives the truest color reproduction;
kraft mutes everything and shifts it warm, which is a feature if you design for it and a
problem if you do not. Coated stock takes gloss and spot UV better than uncoated. If print
