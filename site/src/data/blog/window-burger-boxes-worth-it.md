A window box has a die-cut panel in the lid so the food shows without the box being opened.
It photographs well and sells well on a counter — and it is not free, structurally or
practically.

## What a window actually buys you

Three things, and they are all about the moment before the box is opened. On a display
counter, a window turns packaging into a display case. In a delivery photograph, it shows the
customer what they ordered without a staged shot. And at handover it removes the check —
staff and customer can both see the order is right without breaking the seal.

If none of those moments exists in your service, the window is decoration.

## What it costs structurally

Cutting a panel out of the lid removes material from the part of the box that resists
compression. A window box is measurably weaker under a stack than the same box without one,
which matters most in exactly the situation where the window is most attractive: delivery,
where boxes are stacked in a bag.

If you want a window on a delivery box, put the strength back with a stiffer board — the
options are compared under
[burger boxes by material](/product-category/boxes-by-material/).

## Heat and steam

A window is also a hole, unless it is filmed. Unfilmed, it vents — which keeps fried items
crisp and lets heat escape faster than a closed box. Filmed, it holds heat but traps steam
against the film, where it condenses and fogs, and a fogged window shows the customer
nothing.

That fogging is the most common disappointment with window boxes and it is predictable: hot,
moist food behind film will fog. Fried, drier items behind film generally will not.

## Grease at the edge

The cut edge of a window is exposed board, and exposed board is where grease wicks fastest.
On a wet build, a window box will show a mark around the opening before a closed box shows
anything. A liner under the build fixes it more cheaply than a heavier box does — the
options are in our guide to
[greaseproof burger packaging](/greaseproof-burger-packaging/).

## Where it pays

Window boxes earn their cost on display counters, on grab-and-go shelves where the customer
chooses from what they can see, for gift and multi-item boxes where the arrangement is part
of the product, and for anything dry and photogenic.

They are hardest to justify for a single hot burger going straight into a delivery bag,
where the window weakens the box, fogs, and is never seen by anyone before it is opened.

## The alternative

If the goal is that the packaging communicates rather than that the food is visible, print
usually does it better and costs less. A well-printed closed box survives the stack, holds
heat and never fogs — the range is under
