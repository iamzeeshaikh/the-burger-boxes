"Eco-friendly" is doing a lot of work on a lot of packaging pages, including some that
mean very little by it. The terms are not interchangeable, and choosing well starts with
knowing which claim you are actually making.

## Recyclable, biodegradable, compostable

Recyclable means the material can be reprocessed if it reaches the right facility — and
board that is heavily coated in plastic often cannot be, whatever the base material was.
Biodegradable means it breaks down eventually, which on its own is a weak claim because it
says nothing about how long or under what conditions. Compostable is the strictest of the
three: it breaks down into material that does not harm the compost, within a defined
process.

A box can be recyclable and not compostable, or biodegradable and useless in practice. If
you are going to put the claim on the packaging, it is worth knowing which one you are
entitled to make.

## Bagasse

Bagasse is the fiber left over after sugarcane has been milled for sugar — a by-product
that would otherwise be waste. Pressed into packaging it is sturdy, holds heat reasonably
well, and resists grease structurally rather than through a plastic lining, which is the
detail that matters: it can compost precisely because it does not need the coating that
would stop it.

## Recycled and unbleached kraft

Recycled kraft uses reclaimed fiber, which lowers the input cost of the material in
environmental terms. Plain kraft is unbleached, which skips a processing stage
altogether — that is why it is brown. Neither is as strong a claim as compostable, but
both are honest, cheap, and visible: brown board signals the intent without a word of
copy, which is part of why it became the default look for independents.

## Where coatings complicate it

The coating is usually what decides whether board is recyclable in practice. A heavy
plastic laminate makes a box hold grease beautifully and makes it much harder to
reprocess. If recyclability is the claim you want to make, keep the coating light, put the
grease barrier inside the box as a liner or wrap instead, and check the trade-offs in our
guide to [greaseproof burger packaging](/greaseproof-burger-packaging/).

## Print, ink and honesty

Less print is generally better here, and it is also cheaper. A single-color logo on kraft
uses less ink, costs less to run, and looks more consistent with the claim than full-color
artwork over the whole box. If you want the option later, colored stock is a middle
ground — the range is under
[burger boxes by color](/product-category/boxes-by-color/).

## Choosing without greenwashing

Pick the strongest claim you can actually stand behind, then say exactly that on the box.
"Compostable" on bagasse is a real statement. "Eco-friendly" on plastic-laminated board is
the kind of thing customers increasingly check, and being caught out costs more than the
packaging saved.
